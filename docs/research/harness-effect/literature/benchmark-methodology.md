# Methodology for evaluating agent harnesses: contamination, variance, power, and null results

Scope: literature notes for a project that (1) makes a configuration layer around LLM coding agents (instruction files, hooks, skill libraries, sub-agent routing, stop gates) functionally better, and (2) tests whether such layers change measured outcomes: correctness, false completion claims, cost, wall time. This file covers evaluation methodology only: contamination, variance and error bars, sample sizes, held-out tests, preregistration, and publishing null results in software engineering venues. All citations below were fetched and read via the listed URLs; nothing is cited from memory.

## Synthesis

What the sources jointly support. First, single-run agent benchmark numbers have a noise floor large enough to invalidate small claims of progress. Across 60,000 trajectories on SWE-bench Verified, per-run resolution rates span 2.2 to 6.0 percentage points and standard deviations exceed 1.5 points even at temperature 0, so a reported 2 to 3 point gain may be sampling noise [2]. Benchmark-level variance is a general, under-measured phenomenon [9], and prompt wording alone moves results across 6.5M instances [10]. The statistical machinery to handle this exists and is mature: clustered standard errors (up to 3x inflation when ignored), paired per-question differences, and power analysis that says roughly 1,000 questions are needed to detect a 3 point difference at 80% power, or roughly 9 to 36 repeated runs per configuration to detect 2 to 1 point differences on a 500-task suite [1][2].

Second, measured agent outcomes are inflated by instrument defects, not just noise. Solution leakage in issue text explains 32.67% of SWE-bench successes and 31.08% of passes rest on weak tests, cutting one agent's headline rate from 12.47% to 3.97% [6]; a trivial empty-response agent passes 38% of tau-bench airline tasks; trivial agents can score 100% on SWE-Lancer; and unit-test-only grading overestimates KernelBench by 31 points [4]. Contamination is structural: over 94% of SWE-bench issues predate model cutoffs [6], which is why live collection of post-release problems is the credible design [7]. Public leaderboards add selective-disclosure distortions on top, with private variant testing and score retraction [8].

Third, cost and failure behavior must be measured alongside accuracy, and compute escalation is not a free win. Simple retry, warming and escalation baselines match or beat famous agent architectures on HumanEval at a fraction of the cost (up to 50x cheaper than LATS), and joint cost-accuracy optimization cut variable cost 41 to 53 percent at equal accuracy [3]. HAL's standardized 21,730-rollout study found higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in the majority of runs, and its log inspection surfaced reward-hacking behaviors invisible to accuracy metrics, such as agents searching for the benchmark itself [5]. Most agent benchmarks lack held-out sets appropriate to their claimed generality, enabling shortcut agents [3]; AgentBench's failure taxonomy (instruction following, long-horizon reasoning) is the complement: what agents fail at when the instrument is honest [11].

Fourth, process remedies exist and are entering SE practice: preregistration templates for AI-agent experiments [12], preregistered protocols already accepted at ESEM for exactly the kind of configuration-layer questions this project studies [13], registered reports tracks at MSR [15], and replication and negative-results tracks at ICSME and other venues that publish null results as first-class output [14].

What the sources contradict or tension with each other. Temperature guidance looks contradictory but is not: [1] argues against lowering temperature to shrink variance (it can bias the mean and shift variance into unremovable components), while [2] shows temperature 1.0 versus 0.0 changes DeepSWE-preview's mean success rate by 11 points. The resolution is that temperature is a treatment variable when studying behavior and a nuisance only within a fixed configuration; both agree it never buys statistical determinism. Second, [3] shows complex scaffolds lose to simple baselines on HumanEval, while [4] shows HumanEval-style unit-test grading is itself an unreliable instrument; so a scaffold winning on a weak instrument is weaker evidence than either paper alone suggests. Third, [2] reports a noise floor of 1.5 to 1.8 percentage points on SWE-bench Verified, whereas many model-card claims of 2 to 3 point improvements persist; [6] and [4] additionally show that even the "verified" version of SWE-bench retains leakage and weak-test failures, so the reported leaderboards combine sampling noise with instrument bias. Fourth, the common routing heuristic "send hard tasks to bigger models with more reasoning" is directly contradicted by HAL's finding that higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in most runs [5], and indirectly by [3]'s finding that escalation baselines are the strongest cheap competitor.

Open gaps. No fetched source measures the effect of instruction-file content or length (CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md) on outcomes; the only registered study addressing configuration mechanisms is a protocol with results pending [13]. No source quantifies the variance contributed by lifecycle hooks or stop gates themselves, though hook-level reruns inherit the trajectory-level nondeterminism documented in [2]. Wall time appears in [3] as a recorded cost but is not analyzed as a primary outcome anywhere. Contamination remediation is mature for code contests [7] but untested for private, repo-specific task banks. Preregistration in SE is institutionally young: the ESEM and MSR tracks are 2026-era phenomena [13][15], and there is no standard yet for reporting a null result about a harness configuration change. Finally, the pass@k versus pass^k envelope [2] has no equivalent "completion-claim reliability" metric in the harness literature, which is the gap a stop gate would have to fill.

## What a configuration layer should do differently

These recommendations are what the methodology literature justifies for a layer that wraps LLM coding agents. Each is tied to the entries that support it.

Stop gates should be power-aware multi-run instruments, not single green checks. A gate that declares a task complete after one passing test run inherits the noise floor of the benchmark: single-run estimates vary by 2.2 to 6.0 points with sigma above 1.5 points even at temperature 0 [2]. Concretely: the gate should re-run the touched test files k times (k = 3 to 5) and require all passes (the pass^k criterion) before allowing a completion claim, and it should reserve the pass@k interpretation as an optimistic bound, never as evidence of reliability [2]. When the layer is used to compare two harness configurations, the gate should compute paired per-question differences on a fixed task set, cluster standard errors by repository, and refuse to report a comparison without question counts and standard errors, per the reporting format of [1]. The decision rule for how many reruns a comparison needs is already tabulated: about 9 runs per configuration to detect a 2 point improvement at 80% power, about 36 for 1 point, given the observed sigma of 1.5% [2].

Never tune sampling temperature or other inference settings to make a gate pass. Lowering temperature does not create determinism and can shift variance into the question-difficulty component where resampling cannot remove it, or move the mean outright [1][2]. If a gate fails at the vendor-recommended temperature, that is a result to record, not a parameter to adjust.

Treat every instruction-file edit as an experiment with paired measurement, and keep the file minimal. Single-prompt results are brittle across 6.5M instances [10], so a CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md line that helps one task class can silently hurt another. Mechanism: the layer should version instruction files, evaluate any change as a paired per-task A/B against the incumbent on a fixed task bank, and merge only if the paired difference excludes zero [1]. No fetched source gives an optimal length; the defensible, literature-grounded rule is that every additional line must pay for itself in a paired comparison, because unmeasured additions are indistinguishable from the prompt perturbations that scramble scores [10]. Length-matched placebo controls should be used when testing whether a skill's content or merely its structure is responsible for a change, mirroring the checklist discipline of rigorous benchmark construction [4].

Keep task-specific solutions out of instruction files and skills, and evaluate on post-cutoff tasks. A skill that encodes the answer to known benchmark-style tasks produces solution leakage of exactly the kind that inflated SWE-bench by 32.67% and that live benchmarks are designed to eliminate [6][7]. The layer should lint CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md and skill bodies for benchmark-derived specifics, and its evaluation harness should draw tasks published after the model's training cutoff, stratified by date as LiveCodeBench does [7]. A private held-out task bank, refreshed on a schedule, is the harness-side counterpart [3][7].

Gate every new hook, skill, or routing rule on a cost-adjusted Pareto comparison against trivial baselines. Before adopting a complex scaffold change, run the retry and escalation baselines on the same tasks and require the change to sit on the accuracy-versus-dollar Pareto frontier, over at least 5 runs per configuration [3]. Report tokens and dollars per task next to accuracy, since accuracy-only reporting has produced a literature where costs differ by two orders of magnitude at similar accuracy [3][5].

Do not delegate upward by default. Routing to larger models or higher reasoning effort must be validated per task class, because higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in the majority of HAL's runs [5], and the escalation baseline is often the cheapest way to gain accuracy [3]. The layer's router should log per-task model tier, cost, and outcome so routing rules can be audited as paired comparisons rather than asserted.

Harden the outcome validity of the gate itself. Passing the repository's test suite is not completion: weak and leaky tests produce false positives at the rates documented in [4] and [6]. Mechanism: before trusting a green run, the gate should occasionally inject a known fault into the changed code and confirm the suite fails (a cheap mutation check); it should reject trivially satisfiable completion conditions; and it should run LLM-aided inspection of agent logs for reward hacking patterns like benchmark search, since such behaviors are invisible to accuracy metrics [4][5]. A completion claim that survives k reruns [2] plus a fault-injection check on the suite is categorically stronger evidence than a single pass.

Preregister harness experiments, and design them so the null is publishable. The layer should maintain a registry: hypothesis, primary metric, minimum detectable effect, sample size from the formulas in [1][2], analysis plan, and frozen task bank, committed before rollout, following the degrees-of-freedom catalog in [12]. For results intended for publication, use the SE registered report route already demonstrated for configuration studies of Claude Code and Codex [13] and the MSR track [15], and target replication and negative-results tracks when the effect does not replicate [14]. Reporting should include n, standard errors, cluster counts, and pairwise differences with correlations as the default table shape [1].

## Claims to test

Each experiment below is stated with a measurable outcome and a rough sample size; sample sizes derive from the power numbers in [1] and [2].

1. Variance census of the harness. Run one fixed 100-task held-out set, one configuration, 10 independent runs at the vendor temperature and 10 at temperature 0 (about 2,000 agent runs). Outcome: the per-run resolution-rate distribution, its standard deviation, and the temperature effect with a paired test. This calibrates the minimum detectable effect for every later experiment; the prior is sigma between 0.7 and 1.8 percentage points with a possible temperature effect of up to 11 points on some models and near zero on others [2].

2. False completion rate of the stop gate. Take tasks the gate passes once (target 200) and re-run each 5 times (about 1,000 runs). Outcome: the fraction of completion claims that fail at least one rerun, and the fraction that survive pass^5. This measures the gate's false-claim rate directly; the trajectory-divergence mechanism predicts early divergence within the first percent of tokens, so wall-clock length of the task should be recorded as a covariate [2].

3. Instruction-file ablation with paired analysis. Four arms: no instruction file, curated short file (under 50 lines), curated long file, and a length-matched placebo of filler guidance. Paired per-task differences on a fixed bank; to detect a 3 point absolute difference at 80% power requires roughly 1,000 tasks per the sample-size formula [1]; a 300-task pilot detects effects of about 5 to 6 points. Outcomes: paired mean difference with 95% confidence interval per arm pair, cost per task, and whether the placebo arm moves the metric at all (the placebo result separates structure from content).

4. Routing and escalation policy comparison. Four arms: always cheap model, always frontier model, escalate-on-test-failure, escalate-on-difficulty-classifier, each on 200 tasks with 5 runs (about 4,000 runs). Outcomes: accuracy, dollars per task, and wall time per task, plotted as a Pareto curve against the retry baseline [3]; plus the sign of the reasoning-effort effect on this task distribution to check HAL's finding that more reasoning often hurt [5].

5. Contamination sweep of the task bank. Stratify 300 tasks into 150 published before and 150 after the model's cutoff date. Outcome: the before-minus-after score gap; a large gap indicates the bank leaks into training data and disqualifies those tasks for harness comparisons, following the live-benchmark design principle [6][7].

6. Preregistered null on one plausible skill. Choose one widely promoted prompt-skill pattern, preregister the hypothesis, minimum detectable effect, sample size (about 1,000 paired tasks for a 3 point effect, per [1]), and analysis plan in the layer's registry before running. Outcome: publish the result either way, through a registered report track at ESEM or MSR [13][15] or a replication and negative-results track [14]. The deliverable is both the effect estimate and a worked demonstration that the harness pipeline can produce a publishable null.
