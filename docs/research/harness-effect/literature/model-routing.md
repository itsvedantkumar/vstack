# Model routing and cascades: when cheap-first reduces cost without reducing quality, and what it needs to work

Scope: This note compiles verified literature on LLM routing and cascades, cheap model first and expensive model on demand, with emphasis on 2024 to 2026 results for agentic coding. It serves two purposes: making a configuration layer around coding agents functionally better, and grounding a paper on whether such layers change measured correctness, false completion claims, cost, and wall time.

## Sources

1. Chen, Zaharia, Zou (2023). FrugalGPT: How to Use Large Language Models While Reducing Cost and Improving Performance. arXiv 2305.05176.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176
Claim: A three-stage cascade (GPT-J, then J1-L, then GPT-4) with a DistilBERT generation scorer matches GPT-4 at a fraction of the cost. "FrugalGPT can match the performance of the best individual LLM (e.g. GPT-4) with up to 98% cost reduction or improve the accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% with the same cost" (abstract). Table 3 gives per-dataset cost savings to match the best single model: 98.3% on HEADLINES, 73.3% on OVERRULING, 59.2% on COQA. The learned thresholds in the case study are 0.96 for the first stage and 0.37 for the second (Section 4, case study). Price heterogeneity is the premise: "to process 10M input tokens, GPT-J from Textsynth costs only $0.2, but OpenAI's GPT-4 needs $30" (Table 1). Cheap-to-expensive complementarity is quantified: "for about 6% of the data, GPT-4 makes a mistake but GPJ-J (or J-L or GPT-C) gives the right answer on HEADLINES" (Section 4, LLM diversity), and 13% on COQA for GPT-3 over GPT-4.
Method and sample: cascade optimization over 12 commercial APIs and 3 datasets (10,000, 2,400, and 7,982 examples) with train/test splits; a DistilBERT regression scorer; learned router list and thresholds.
Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/html/2305.05176v1 (full text). Confidence: high.

2. Ong, Almahairi, Wu, Chiang, Wu, Gonzalez, Kadous, Stoica (2024, v4 2025). RouteLLM: Learning to Route LLMs with Preference Data. arXiv 2406.18665.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665
Claim: A router trained on Chatbot Arena preferences routes between GPT-4 and Mixtral 8x7B, cutting cost over 2x at matched quality; the best routers reach 3.66x cost savings on MT Bench at 95% of GPT-4 quality (Table 6; MMLU 1.41x at 92%, GSM8K 1.49x at 87%). Training data distribution is the binding constraint: "Routers trained only on D_arena perform poorly due to most questions being out-of-distribution, but dataset augmentation with D_gold is highly effective" (Table 2 note); a benchmark-dataset similarity score tracks router performance (Table 5). Routers transfer across model pairs without retraining: on MT Bench the SW ranking router improves APGR by 56.6% over random for Claude 3 Opus versus Sonnet and 49.8% for Llama 3.1 70B versus 8B (Table 4). Router overhead is small: the most expensive router "adds an extra cost of no more than 0.4% as compared to GPT-4 generation" (Table 7 and Section 5.5). Data augmentation matters: the matrix factorization router's CPT(50%) on MT Bench drops from 25.32% of calls to 13.40% with GPT-4-judge-augmented data (Table 1).
Method and sample: 80k Chatbot Arena battles (65k usable comparisons, 64 models), four router architectures, evaluation on MMLU (14,042 questions), MT Bench (160 questions), GSM8K (1,319 problems).
Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18665v4 (full text). Confidence: high.

3. Hu, Bieker, Li, Jiang, Keigwin, Ranganath, Keutzer, Upadhyay (2024). RouterBench: A Benchmark for Multi-LLM Routing System. arXiv 2403.12031.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12031
Claim: Price-performance headroom is real but bounded, and verifier quality gates cascades. "The monetary costs of LLM services can routinely vary by factors of 2-5x for comparable levels of performance" (Section 1, contribution 3). The oracle router rarely picks GPT-4, since cheaper models answer most queries correctly (Section 4.4 and Figure 3 heatmap). Simple predictive routers (KNN, MLP) do not clearly beat a Zero router that interpolates the per-model convex hull: they exceed it on MMLU and Winogrande, match it on HellaSwag and GSM8K, and underperform on ARC-Challenge and MBPP (Section 5.3). Cascade results are the sharpest: "cascading routers surpass both individual LLMs and the Zero router by a significant margin when the router's judge has an error rate of up to 0.1. However, as the judge's error rates increase, the performance of the cascading router may deteriorate rapidly, particularly when the error rate exceeds 0.2" (Section 5.3).
Method and sample: 405,467 recorded inference outcomes over 11 models and 8 datasets including MBPP and GSM8K, plus a 800-query RAG dataset where routers learn to send time-sensitive queries to online models (Section 5.4).
Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12031v2 (full text). Confidence: high.

4. Ding, Mallick, Wang, Sim, Mukherjee, Ruhle, Lakshmanan, Awadallah (2024). Hybrid LLM: Cost-Efficient and Quality-Aware Query Routing. ICLR 2024, arXiv 2404.14618.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14618
Claim: A DeBERTa router predicting the probability that the small model matches the large model routes queries pre-answer, single call. "Our approach allows us to make up to 40% fewer calls to the large model, with no drop in response quality" (abstract). The gain depends on the performance gap between the pair: for Llama-2 13b versus GPT-3.5-turbo (medium gap), 40% cost advantage costs 2.9% BART-score drop with the probabilistic router plus label transformation, and 20% advantage costs 0.8% (Table 1); for same-family pairs (Llama-2 7b versus 13b) 40% advantage costs 0.0%. When the small model is far weaker (FLAN-t5 800m versus Llama-2 13b), plain routers fail and the transformed router still gets "40% cost advantages with 10.3% quality drop" (Section 4.2). Non-determinism is modeled directly: training labels are probabilities estimated from 10 samples per model per query (Section 3.2).
Method and sample: MixInstruct, 10k training queries with 10 responses per model each, 5k validation and 5k test; three model-pair gap regimes; threshold tuned at test time to trade quality for cost.
Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/html/2404.14618v1 (full text). Confidence: high.

5. Aggarwal, Madaan, Anand, Potharaju, Mishra, Zhou, Gupta, Rajagopal, Kappaganthu, Yang, Upadhyay, Faruqui, Mausam (2024). AutoMix: Automatically Mixing Language Models. NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2310.12963.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12963
Claim: Verify-then-route beats route-then-generate in the cascade setting. A few-shot self-verification prompt, framed as entailment between the answer and the context, produces a reliability signal; a POMDP router consumes the noisy signal and decides whether to escalate. "reducing computational cost by over 50% for comparable performance" across five models and five datasets (abstract). The POMDP learns when not to escalate at all: it "identifies that lower confidence values correspond to such cases, and instead of routing to the LLM as other methods do, returns the SLM answer, saving cost" on likely unsolvable queries (Section 5.4). It is data cheap: "with just 50 training examples, AutoMix maintains 15% Delta-ibc and beats the baselines by more than absolute 20%" (Section 5.4), and cost-effective at modest price ratios: "even for a cost ratio as low as 1:10, AutoMix begins to deliver good gains" (Section 5.4). It outperforms FrugalGPT and HybridLLM routers on the incremental-benefit-per-cost metric across all five datasets for most model pairs (Table 1).
Method and sample: five datasets (QASPER, QuALITY, CoQA, MuTual, DIPLOMAT), three small models (gpt-3.5, llama2-13b, mistral-7b) against GPT-4, with cost ratios 1:60 to 1:200.
Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12963v5 (full text). Confidence: high.

6. Bhola, Krishnan, Mukunda NS (2026). Scrouting: Cost-Aware Routing of Coding Agents by Scouting the Repository First. arXiv 2608.04804.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04804
Claim: On repository-level issue solving, accuracy routing has almost no headroom because solve sets are nested: "the overlap is 0.941, 0.912, and 0.773 across the three benchmarks" (SWE-bench Verified, Multilingual, Pro; Section 3.2), and "no learned router we tested exceeded the solve rate of always picking the strongest model; every observed gap fell within noise" (Section 3.2, a zero-cost replay audit over published per-task results). The cheapest tier can dominate outright: "Kimi K2.5 solo resolves 149 of 266 tasks (56.02%) at $0.106 per task, dominating GPT-5.2 solo (139/266, 52.26%, $0.570 per task) on both axes" (Section 7.1).
Method and sample: census of the 266-task Python slice of SWE-bench Pro under a 50-call and $2.00 per-attempt cap, four fixer models, all-in cost accounting including a 7B searcher whose total GPU bill was $1.13; a paired calibration study on 99 fresh tasks for the redistribution mechanism.

## Synthesis

What the sources support. Routing saves cost at matched quality when two conditions hold. First, there is a real price-quality spread to exploit, from two orders of magnitude in early 2023 API pricing [1] to the 2 to 5x for comparable performance that RouterBench measures across eleven models [3]. Second, the routing signal is good enough. The best-documented signals are post-generation: a trained scorer of the cheap model's answer [1], few-shot self-verification consumed by a POMDP [5], executed tests in a sandbox [6], token confidence plus user rejection [7]. Pre-answer routers also work but only in-domain: RouteLLM's routers trained on Arena data are indistinguishable from random on MMLU and GSM8K until augmented with a small amount of task-relevant labeled data, and its benchmark-dataset similarity score predicts where a router will work [2]. Hybrid LLM shows the same dependence from the other side: routing gains shrink monotonically as the small-to-large capability gap widens, and a router trained naively in the large-gap regime barely beats random assignment [4].

What the sources contradict. The 2024 generation of router papers reports large gains over best-single-model baselines [1,2,4,5]; the 2026 diagnostics report that those gains shrink to noise once evaluation is made statistically honest. With selection-valid intervals, the best of eleven router policies is not certifiably better than the best fixed model on any of four benchmarks, even though certified oracle opportunity of 9.7 to 30.7 points exists [9]. The task-type decomposition goes further: after a double-run reproducibility floor of 5.37%, only 29 of 294 questions are routable at all, and a static per-task-type table captures 21 of them, beating the best single model at less than half the cost [8]. Three reconciliations are visible in the sources themselves. RouteLLM admits its OOD weakness and shows the fix is in-domain data, so part of the early gains were train-test similarity [2]. RouterBench's own cascading result shows verifier error above 0.2 destroys the cascade, so gains reported with oracle or near-oracle judges do not transfer to real judges [3]. And both diagnostics measure small-model pools on short-answer benchmarks, a setting where the strongest model's solve set nearly subsumes everything [9], which is exactly what the coding-agent audit confirms with containment of 0.77 to 0.94 and zero routers beating always-strongest [6].

The coding-agent results invert the pre-answer routing premise in a productive way. SuperScout's headline cost reduction comes from a verified handoff consumed by a cheap fixer, not from the routing decision, since the no-router ablation ties the routed system [6]. MCCom's gains come from escalation triggered by executed evidence (token confidence, user rejection) and from reusing the cheap model's rejected output to improve the expensive model's context [7]. AutoMix's advantage over route-first baselines comes from its verification step, and its POMDP's distinctive ability is knowing when not to escalate at all [5]. The consistent mechanism across [5,6,7] is: run the cheap tier, gather evidence about whether it worked, escalate only on failed evidence, and hand the evidence to the expensive tier.

On configuration content rather than model choice, the skills study shows that unconditional instruction injection is negative on average, model-specific, and most harmful on tasks the model already solves [10], and the architecture study shows that specification format can substitute for model capability, tripling coverage for the weakest model while barely mattering for the strongest [12]. Together they imply that the configuration layer and the routing layer are coupled: the value of a per-tier instruction file depends on which tier it is attached to.

Open gaps. No source measures the effect of routing or cascade configuration on false claims of completion; SuperScout's verify-then-strip gate is the closest artifact, showing most of the cheap searcher's reproduction claims are false before gating [6], but no one has measured claim-falsity rates across model tiers under different stop gates. Wall time is nearly unmeasured outside interactive completion [7] and router latency tables [2,11]; no source reports end-to-end wall-clock for agentic tasks under routing. Instruction-file length limits have no direct evidence; the skills study measures token overhead (plus 72% to 394%) and length versus content mechanisms but not a dose-response curve for file length [10]. Sub-agent routing across tiers inside a multi-agent harness is untested in the literature; the closest is SuperScout's searcher-fix split and the SWE-Router contrast cited there [6]. The negative diagnostics [8,9] are benchmark-bound (short-answer tasks, one coding cell with no contamination control [8]), so they bound claims rather than settle the agentic setting.

## What a configuration layer should do differently

Route on executed evidence, not on prompt features, whenever execution is available. The strongest quantitative result on routing signal quality is RouterBench's: cascades beat every alternative only while the judge's error rate stays under about 0.1 and deteriorate rapidly above 0.2 [3]. In a coding agent, the judge can be the test suite, which has near-zero error for the claims it covers. The layer should therefore make a cheap-tier attempt run the tests, and treat a green run as acceptance and a red run as the escalation trigger, which is the structure that works in [1,5,6,7]. Prompt-only routers should be reserved for workloads where execution feedback is unavailable, and then only after the in-domain calibration that RouteLLM shows is mandatory [2].

Do not delegate hard coding tasks for accuracy reasons; delegate for cost with verified evidence. The replay audit shows no router beats always-strongest on accuracy across three SWE benchmarks, with solve-set containment of 0.77 to 0.94 [6], and the selection-valid analysis shows best-of-family router gains are statistically indistinguishable from zero even where oracle opportunity is large [9]. The configuration layer's router, if any, should be a cost router: predict when the cheap tier will suffice, and measure that prediction against the cheap tier's own test results. A static task-type-conditioned table is a justified default before any learned router: it captured 21 of 29 routable items and halved cost in the one decomposition study available [8], and the corresponding claim should be re-estimated per repository since that study is in-sample on 294 items [8].

Gate sub-agent and cheap-tier claims through execution before they propagate upward. "most reproduction claims are in fact false" before the verify-then-strip gate [6] is the direct literature counterpart of the false-completion-claim problem. The mechanism is cheap: replay the claimed failing test in a sandbox, strip claims that do not reproduce, and only then hand the artifact to the next tier or to the human. The same gate should wrap any stop condition that would otherwise let a session end on an unverified claim.

Enforce budgets at the workload level and re-solve them adaptively. Per-query budget logic either fails outright under tight budgets, where FrugalGPT-style cascades score zero on both benchmarks in the tightest conditions [11], or overshoots by more than 5% when the constraint is static [11]. The layer should meter cumulative spend per session, sprint, or subscription period and re-solve the cheap-to-expensive allocation as the remaining budget shrinks, in the pattern of adaptive linear programming [11], with conformal bounds on the escalation rate where a guarantee is wanted [13].

Preserve the cheap tier's work across escalation. Restarting from scratch on escalation discards the only asset the cheap run produced. MCCom reuses the rejected completion as a speculative draft and as a retrieval query, improving the large model's exact match by 8.9% on average [7]; SuperScout's fixer consumes a verified handoff of implicated files, diagnostics, and a repro test [6]. The configuration layer should define a handoff artifact format and a verification step for it, so that escalation is incremental rather than restart.

Keep instruction files short, opt-in per model, and front-load anti-patterns over examples. Unconditional skill injection lowers Pass@2 by 1.3 to 4.2 points while raising token cost 72% to 394%, gains occur in only 17% to 36% of pairs, and harm concentrates on easy tasks [10]. Anti-pattern rules are the only slice with a reliable positive effect while example code is null pooled and hurts the strongest model by 15.3 points [10]. A practical policy follows: no skill or long instruction file attached at session start by default; attach per (model, project) pair after a paired audit; prefer proscriptive rules over examples for frontier tiers; and always run a length-matched irrelevant control before adopting a file, since for some models an equally long irrelevant file reproduces most of the loss [10]. Nothing in the literature justifies a specific byte limit, but the 72% to 394% token overhead on 1.2K to 22K character files [10] bounds where to start measuring.

Tie specification weight to model tier, not to task importance. Code-proximate formats (OpenAPI, TypeScript contracts) recover most of the capability gap on weaker models, including tripling API route coverage for the weakest, while adding nothing for the strongest [12]. Combined with [10], the configuration layer should attach heavy interface contracts and validators to cheap-tier delegations and keep frontier-tier prompts lean, since extra prompt material is at best neutral for frontier models and at worst the length-distractor [10]. The compile-debug-loop finding, where mid-tier models spend more tokens than frontier models for worse output [12], also argues for a stop hook that caps repair iterations per tier rather than per task.

Calibrate escalation thresholds on held-out data and certify the router before deploying it. AutoMix trains its router on 50 examples and still beats domain-trained baselines [5], C3PO achieves guarantees with small calibration sets [13], and the selection-valid framework shows how to test whether a router family beats the fixed baseline at all before shipping it [9]. The configuration layer should hold out a labeled slice of real tasks, fit thresholds there, and report a paired interval against the always-strongest baseline, refusing to deploy a learned router whose interval includes zero [9].

When not to delegate: when the pool is nested, when the judge is noisy, and when the budget is per query. Three conditions emerge from [3,6,9,11]: accuracy delegation has no headroom when weaker solve sets are subsumed by the strongest, which is measurable from past per-task outcomes [6]; cascades lose money when judge error exceeds 0.2 [3]; and per-query constrained escalation cannot meet workload budgets [11]. In those regimes the correct configuration is always-strongest with budget metering, or always-cheapest with an execution gate, both of which matched or beat learned routing in the coding setting [6].

## Claims to test

1. Test-gated cascade versus strongest-only on repository issues. Run the same task set under (a) strongest model only and (b) cheap tier with a test-suite stop gate that escalates on failure, passing the trajectory and failing tests to the strong tier. Outcomes: solve rate, cost per solve, wall time, rate of false completion claims (claimed done but failing held-out tests). Sample: 300 tasks x 2 arms x 3 seeds, roughly 1,800 agent runs; powered to detect a 5-point solve-rate difference given run-to-run noise on the order of the 5.37% flip floor [8,6].
2. Verified handoff versus raw handoff for false-claim propagation. Same cheap searcher, handoff either sandbox-verified and stripped [6] or passed through raw. Outcome: downstream solve rate of the fixer and count of stripped false claims. Sample: 200 paired tasks, since the source study was directional at N=99 [6].
3. Static task-type table versus learned router versus always-strongest on a mixed engineering workload. Fit a per-task-type model table on half the workload, evaluate on a held-out half, replicated over two full runs to estimate the reproducibility floor. Outcomes: accuracy, cost per run, fraction of routing decisions that change under re-execution. Sample: 1,000 tasks, 4 task types, 2 runs, following the decomposition design [8] with the holdout it lacked [8].
4. Instruction injection dose-response with length-matched controls. Inject each instruction or skill file at three lengths (full, half, and byte-matched irrelevant control) per model.
