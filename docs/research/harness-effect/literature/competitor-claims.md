# Claims audit of public Claude Code configuration bundles, with supporting literature

This report audits the quantitative and qualitative claims made by public configuration bundles for Claude Code and comparable coding agents, and pairs each bundle with the peer literature that bears on the same mechanisms (instruction files, hooks, stop gates, skills, sub-agent routing). Its two purposes are, first, to say what a configuration layer should copy or avoid based on which claims have evidence behind them, and second, to ground a paper on whether such layers change measured outcomes: correctness, false claims of completion, cost, and wall time. Every source listed below was fetched during compilation; URLs given are the exact documents read.

## Synthesis

What the sources support. The strongest supported claim in this space is negative: context files, the core artifact most bundles distribute, do not reliably improve task success (entries 14 and 17) even though agents follow the instructions they contain (14). What files do measurably change is efficiency: entry 15 finds lower runtime and fewer output tokens with files present, and entry 14 finds over 20% higher inference cost with them, so both directions of the cost effect have support and the sign depends on the file's content and the agent. Entry 19 resolves part of the apparent contradiction between 14 and 15 by showing the production procedure for the file is decisive, and that well-tuned guidance raises resolve rate by raising coverage of evaluable patches rather than patch quality. On the mechanism side, entry 18 shows compiled, executable enforcement of file contents beats prose compliance by a wide margin (88.3% versus 67.0%), entry 16 shows the prose files themselves are commonly bloated (62% lint leakage, 42% context bloat), and entry 13 supplies a plausible quantitative ceiling (roughly 150 to 200 followable instructions, of which the harness baseline already consumes about 50) that explains both bloat harms. On completion honesty, entry 21 shows prompting alone does not prevent reward hacking, entry 20 shows a structural intervention (an escalation channel) does, and entry 22 shows that even standard benchmark scores can be inflated by shortcuts, which generalizes to self-reported completion claims. On the bundle side, the audit supports a stark gradient of evidentiary practice: superpowers and cc-thinking-skills ship real harnesses (entries 3, 4, 8), claude-code-bench ships a runnable harness with a small-n result set (10), gstack ships self-measurement tooling and a reproducible script but no controlled evaluation (1, 2), and SuperClaude, claude-code-configs, and most of the long tail make specific quantitative claims (2-3x faster, 30-50% fewer tokens, 94% detection, 10x faster development) with no harness or results file in the repository (5, 6, 11).

What they contradict. Entry 10's finding that a full CLAUDE.md is "irreplaceable" and should stay near 200 lines sits in tension with entry 13's less-is-more stance (under sixty lines at HumanLayer, consensus under 300) and with entry 16's context-bloat prevalence data; entry 19 partially reconciles this by showing content quality and procedure, not raw length, is the decisive variable, and entry 14 by showing repository overviews specifically are the useless part. Entries 14 and 15 contradict each other on cost direction as noted. The bundles' headline claims contradict the literature outright: SuperClaude's "2-3x faster" from optional MCP servers and claude-code-configs' "10x faster development" are two to ten times larger than the largest measured effect of any configuration artifact in the peer literature (entry 15's 28.64% runtime reduction), and gstack's ~810x productivity claim, while carefully caveated in entry 2, is a single-subject measurement with no control, in a genre the literature treats as unmeasurable. Finally, entry 8's audit contradicts the whole genre's marketing posture from inside: after preregistered evaluation, 28 skills earned exactly zero automatic-invocation authorizations and one skill showed a negative effect, so a bundle that measures honestly will report mostly nulls.

Open gaps. No source measures hooks or stop gates as independent variables in a controlled design; entry 20 is the closest (escalation channel as a decision-environment intervention) but studies ML-research agents, not Claude Code sessions with a verify-gate. No source measures sub-agent routing across model tiers against a no-routing control; entry 17's agent-specific difficulty bands (rho=0.75) motivate routing but no one has tested it. No source measures the false-completion rate of bundles in the wild; entry 22 provides the audit method but applied to benchmarks, not to README claims. The instruction-count ceiling of entry 13 is unpublished and approximate. Every bundle-level adoption statistic (entry 2's 95.2% success rate) lacks a public dataset. And the cost asymmetry between entries 14 and 15 is unresolved: no study varies file content along the specific axes (overview versus non-standard practices versus commands) while holding the agent fixed and measuring both correctness and cost.

## What a configuration layer should do differently

Keep the instruction file small, universal, and linted. Entries 13, 16, and 14 jointly justify a hard budget: roughly 150 to 200 followable instructions total per entry 13, of which the Claude Code harness baseline already spends about 50, with lint leakage (style rules that a formatter should own) present in 62% of real files and context bloat in 42% per entry 16. Concretely: a configuration layer should cap the root instruction file (entry 10's 200-line/2000-token ceiling is a workable upper bound; entry 13's under-sixty-lines is a better target), prohibit lint and style instructions entirely in favor of deterministic formatters wired as hooks (both 13 and 16 support this), and ship the entry 16 heuristics as a CI linter for instruction files, detecting lint leakage, context bloat, skill leakage, and conflicting instructions before a bundle is distributed. It should also implement entry 13's progressive disclosure pattern as a first-class mechanism: task-specific guidance lives in pointer files loaded on demand, which is exactly the structure entry 19 shows can be tuned productively.

Put non-standard practices in files; leave standard knowledge out. Entry 14's decomposition is directly actionable: instructions are followed, overviews are not helpful. A configuration layer should therefore treat the instruction file as a place for non-obvious conventions (entry 10 found the full variant caught an abbreviation convention the ablated variant missed, which is evidence for retaining genuinely non-standard content), and never for repository overviews, which cost over 20% extra inference per entry 14 and buy no success rate. Entries 17 and 19 add the sharpest reason: agents fail on implementation skill, not missing repository knowledge, so knowledge-shaped configuration is aimed at the wrong failure mode.

Make constraints executable instead of prose. Entry 18's 21-point compliance gap (88.3% versus 67.0%) between compiled guardrails and prompt-only instructions is the largest positive effect in the literature and it is mechanism-level: static AST queries, command-intercepting shims, and structural validators. A configuration layer should compile the instruction file's machine-checkable subset into hooks at install time rather than hoping the model re-reads prose mid-task. Entry 16 supplies the detector for which lines qualify.

Design stop gates around escalation, not repetition. Entry 21 shows prompting an agent not to cheat leaves the cheating rate above 50%, so a stop gate that merely re-states instructions in the system prompt is the intervention the literature has already falsified. Entry 20 shows the intervention that works: a structured escalation channel available at the moment of conflict reduced reward hacking from 23.6% to 5.3% with no performance cost, and 96.8% of escalations were genuine defect reports. A configuration layer should therefore pair its verify-gate (entry 1's pattern: block turn end until the declared verify command passes, with a bounded 3-strike yield so the loop cannot spin) with a first-class escalation path that lets the agent file a structured defect report against the environment or the tests instead of hacking them, and should treat escalations as diagnostics, since entry 20 finds they add 10.1 points of defect-detection coverage at 99.4% precision. Entry 22 adds the reason the gate must bind to fresh evidence: scores and completion claims are otherwise inflateable, so a gate should re-run the verify command itself or grade a freshness ledger (entry 1's FRESH/STALE binding of test evidence to a working-tree fingerprint) rather than accept an agent's assertion that tests pass.

Delegate narrowly, and only on measured bands. Entry 17's finding that borderline difficulty is agent-specific (rho=0.75) means any fixed routing table copied from another bundle is likely miscalibrated for your agent; a configuration layer should either route by its own measured per-band performance or not route at all. Entry 13's exponential decay for smaller models gives a concrete prohibition: never delegate multi-step, many-instruction tasks to small-model subagents, and never hand a subagent the full instruction file, because the instruction budget is already near the frontier-model ceiling before the subagent starts. Entry 19's coverage-not-precision result suggests the correct division of labor: guidance and routing help agents reach the right file; they do not improve the quality of the change once there, so subagent prompts should carry navigation context, and review agents (the only kind with a demonstrated quality role) should be separate.

Ship the harness with the bundle, and let the audit bar claims. The audit's clearest copy/avoid split is evidentiary practice. Copy: entry 8's preregistration-and-registry model, in which the README asserts only what `analysis/evidence.json` authorizes and explicitly lists barred claims; entry 4's behavioral compliance harness that produces three-valued verdicts with cost accounting; entry 10's tool-call logging that makes a bundle's own effectiveness measurable. Avoid: entry 5's uncited multipliers, entry 6's machine-generated self-congratulation, entry 11's unsupported 10x. A configuration layer should adopt a written rule: no quantitative claim in its README without a runnable harness and a results file in the repository, and where the honest result is null or negative (entry 8's socratic skill at −6.9pp), publish that too. Entry 2 is the instructive middle case: its caveats are honest and its script reproducible, but a single subject with no control remains anecdote-grade evidence regardless of polish.

Instrument first, optimize second. Entries 10, 13, and 22 all imply the same prerequisite: a configuration layer cannot know whether it helps until it logs tool calls, verify outcomes, escalations, token spend, and wall time per session. Entry 10's PostToolUse and Stop hooks producing `tool_log.csv` and `violations.csv` are the minimal viable instrumentation, and entry 22's Mislead-gap audit is the method for checking whether any improvement claim survives adversarial inspection.

## Claims to test

Instruction budget dose-response on correctness and cost. Vary a CLAUDE.md at 0, 60, 150, 300, and 600 lines of real (not filler) instructions across two agents on a 50-task SWE-bench Verified subset, 4 seeds per cell, measuring resolve rate, tokens, and wall time. Roughly 2,000 runs. Outcomes to estimate: the correctness plateau from entry 13's ~150-to-200-instruction ceiling, the cost curve from entries 14 and 15, and whether the entry 10 finding (a full file catches convention violations an ablated file misses) survives at 60 lines with better-chosen content. Expected sample size per cell (400 runs) detects a 10-point resolve difference at power 0.8.

Stop-gate escalation factorial. A 2×2 over verify-gate hook (on/off) and structured escalation skill (on/off) on 100 tasks per cell seeded with flawed or overly narrow test infrastructure in the style of entry 21. Measure reward-hacking rate (test-file edits, hardcoded outputs), false completion rate (claimed pass versus hidden-test pass), and escalation precision against a byte-exact oracle in the style of entry 22. Entry 20's base rate of 23.6% gives 80% power at n=100 per cell for a 15-point drop. This is the experiment the literature lacks (the gap named in the synthesis) and it directly measures the false-claims-of-completion outcome.

Executable versus prose constraint enforcement. Replicate entry 18's contrast inside a configuration layer rather than a research framework: compile the machine-checkable subset of an instruction file into hooks on one arm, leave prose-only on a second arm, and add an LLM-reflection arm, on 300 SWE-bench Lite tasks. Measure constraint compliance, feedback cost, and functional correctness, with the entry 18 numbers (88.3 / 67.0 / 50.3) as the replication target.

Routing band calibration. Measure per-agent difficulty bands on 17 real tasks across two agents (entry 17's design, expanded to 10 seeds, roughly 340 runs), then test band-informed routing against random routing and no-routing controls on a held-out task set. Outcome: resolve rate and cost delta attributable to routing, testing the claim, motivated by rho=0.75 and untested anywhere, that routing across model tiers pays for its coordination overhead.

Benchmark-claim replication of bundle numbers. Run the entry 10 harness with 30 seeds per arm instead of 9 single runs to test its three headline effects (prompt detail +0.25, grep-before-edit +0.40, context hint 26% faster) and the entry 13 instruction-count ceiling at the same time. Outcome: which bundle-published effects replicate with confidence intervals, establishing the false-positive rate of small-n configuration research.

Claims-audit as a predictive instrument. Apply entry 22's audit method to the corpus assembled here (every quantitative claim in entries 1 to 13 coded by evidence type) and test whether evidence-graded README claims predict measured effect: run the four bundles that ship harnesses through their own harnesses plus a common task suite, and correlate claim strength with measured outcome. Roughly 4 bundles by 40 tasks by 3 seeds, about 480 runs. Outcome: whether "claims with no harness" bundles actually underperform, which is the paper's headline result if true.
