# Limits of self-verification and self-repair in code generation

Scope line 1: This report reviews what is known about whether a code-generating model can judge the correctness of its own output and of its own fixes.
Scope line 2: It serves a configuration-layer project (instruction files, hooks, skills, sub-agent routing, stop gates that re-run tests) and a paper on whether such layers change measured outcomes.
Scope line 3: Sources were verified by direct fetch; every number below was read from the cited abstract or full text on arXiv.

## Sources

1. Huang, Chen, Mishra, Zheng, Yu, Song, Zhou (2023). Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet. ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.01798. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798. Claim: intrinsic self-correction, where the model judges its own answer with no external feedback, degrades reasoning accuracy. Exact figures, Table 3 of the full text: on GSM8K, GPT-4 falls from 95.5 (standard prompting) to 91.5 after one self-correction round and 89.0 after two; GPT-3.5 falls from 75.9 to 74.7; on CommonSenseQA GPT-3.5 falls from 75.8 to 38.1; Table 4: Llama-2-70b falls from 62.0 to 36.5 on GSM8K. Section 3.3: "74.7% of the time, GPT-3.5 retains its initial answer" and among changed answers, correct-to-incorrect flips dominate incorrect-to-correct. Section 4, Table 7: multi-agent debate at 9 responses scores 83.0 on GSM8K versus 88.2 for plain self-consistency majority voting at the same budget. Section 5, Table 8: on the Constrained Generation task from Self-Refine, an improved initial prompt scores 81.8 while applying Self-Refine-style self-correction on top of it scores 75.1. Method: GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-2-70b on GSM8K (full 1,319-problem test set for GPT-3.5), CommonSenseQA (1,221 dev questions) and HotpotQA (100 questions), up to two correction rounds. Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798 and full text https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798v2. Confidence: high.

2. Olausson, Inala, Wang, Gao, Solar-Lezama (2023). Is Self-Repair a Silver Bullet for Code Generation? ICLR 2024, arXiv:2306.09896. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896. Claim: self-repair gains at matched compute budget are modest, and the bottleneck is the model's feedback on its own code. Exact figures, section 4.1: for GPT-4 on APPS, drawing 10 initial samples then 1 repair each (20 total) yields a pass rate 1.05x the no-repair baseline pass@20, while drawing 2 initial samples then 10 repair candidates each (22 total) yields 0.97x the baseline pass@22. Section 4.2: replacing Code Llama's feedback with GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 feedback, and GPT-3.5's with GPT-4's, "in every case, the boosted configuration beats both the corresponding i.i.d. baseline and the corresponding self-repair configuration at all budgets." Table 1: repair success with GPT-4's own feedback is 33.30 percent overall versus 52.60 percent with human programmer feedback, a 1.58x gain; on competition-level problems the gap widens to 3.67 versus 14.67 percent. Section 4.3 qualitative: GPT-4 feedback is inaccurate in 32 of 80 cases versus 7 of 80 for humans. Method: CodeLlama-13b-instruct, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on HumanEval (164 problems) and 300 sampled APPS tasks; pass rates bootstrapped over 1,000 repair trees drawn from 50 initial samples per problem; human study with 16 participants writing feedback for 40 failing GPT-4 programs. Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896 and full text https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09896v5. Confidence: high.

3. Madaan et al. (2023). Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback. NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2303.17651. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651. Claim: a single LLM acting as generator, feedback provider and refiner improves output quality: "improving by ~20% absolute on average in task performance" across 7 tasks with GPT-3.5, ChatGPT and GPT-4. Method: 7 tasks including code optimization, dialog response generation, math reasoning, judged by human preference and automatic metrics. Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651. Confidence: medium (abstract only; entry 1 shows a subset of these gains is attributable to instructions folded into the feedback prompt).

4. Gou, Shao, Gong, Shen, Yang, Duan, Chen (2023). CRITIC: Large Language Models Can Self-Correct with Tool-Interactive Critiquing. ICLR 2024, arXiv:2305.11738. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738. Claim: LLMs validate and amend outputs only when an external tool supplies the verification signal: "CRITIC interacts with appropriate tools to evaluate certain aspects of the text, and then revises the output based on the feedback obtained during this validation process"; the abstract "highlights the crucial importance of external feedback". Method: evaluations on free-form question answering, mathematical program synthesis and toxicity reduction with tools such as a search engine and code interpreter. Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738. Confidence: medium (abstract only).

5. Tyen, Mansoor, Carbune, Chen, Mak (2023). LLMs cannot find reasoning errors, but can correct them given the error location. ACL 2024 Findings, arXiv:2311.08516. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08516. Claim: the failure is in mistake localization, not in correction. Exact figures, section 3, Table 4: the best model, GPT-4, reaches only 52.87 overall accuracy at locating the first erroneous step in chain-of-thought traces, and GPT-3.5-Turbo reaches 14.78. Section 4, Table 6: with oracle error location, backtracking improves originally incorrect answers by +18.06 to +43.92 percentage points across 5 tasks while degrading originally correct answers by at most 11.43 points; with a random location the gains shrink. Section 5.1: backtracking becomes net-positive when mistake-finding accuracy reaches roughly 60 to 70 percent. Section 5.2: a small fine-tuned classifier (PaLM 2 Otter) beats prompting the much larger PaLM 2 Unicorn at mistake finding on 4 of 5 tasks. Method: BIG-Bench Mistake, 2,186 PaLM 2 Unicorn traces over 5 tasks (word sorting, tracking shuffled objects, logical deduction, multistep arithmetic, Dyck languages), human annotation with Krippendorff alpha 0.979 to 0.998, five models benchmarked. Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08516 and full text https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08516v3. Confidence: high.

Method: five pre-trained models on HumanEval, MBPP, APPS and CodeContests. Verified: fetched https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397. Confidence: medium (abstract only).

## Synthesis

What the sources support. The literature converges on one mechanism: verification is a separate capability from generation, and it degrades sharply when the verifier is the same model judging the same artifact with no external signal. Entry 1 shows intrinsic self-correction reduces accuracy across four models and three reasoning benchmarks, with GPT-4 losing 6.5 points on GSM8K after two rounds, and shows that multi-agent critique adds nothing over majority voting at the same budget. Entry 5 isolates the cause: models locate the first erroneous step at barely above chance (GPT-4 at 52.87 overall on BIG-Bench Mistake) yet correct a located error reliably (gains of 18 to 44 points given the location). Entry 2 finds the same structure in code: self-repair works only as well as the feedback stage, and replacing self-feedback with stronger-model or human feedback unlocks gains that self-repair alone cannot reach (1.58x with human feedback). Entry 11 confirms a self-repair blind spot specifically on self-produced insecure code. The positive result is equally consistent: whenever an external, executable signal exists, correction works. Execution feedback raises accuracy by up to 12 percent with roughly 10x sample efficiency (entry 8), generated-test selection adds 18.8 points of pass@1 (entry 6), trained execution verifiers add 4.6 to 10.9 points (entry 7), test-based staged flows double pass@5 on competition problems (entry 10), and test-gated verbal reflection reaches 91 percent pass@1 on HumanEval (entry 9). Entries 3 and 4 sit between: self-feedback helps on style and quality tasks where the criteria are subjective or checkable in text, and tool-grounded critique helps when a tool carries the verification load.

What the sources contradict. Entry 3 reports about 20 percent average improvement from same-model feedback and refinement, while entry 1 demonstrates that on constrained generation tasks part of that improvement is an artifact of instructions appearing for the first time in the feedback prompt; with an equally informative initial prompt, self-correction lowers the score (81.8 versus 75.1). Entry 12 reports large self-repair gains (+16.0 to +30.0 points on MBPP) from prompting alone for modern models, which reads as contradicting entry 2's modest matched-budget gains, but the two are compatible once budgets are distinguished: entry 2 charges repair against an equal-sample resampling baseline, while entry 12 measures improvement over single-shot generation, which entry 8 already showed repair can beat by an order of magnitude in sample efficiency. The practical takeaway is that the contradiction is about accounting, not mechanism.

Open gaps. No source measures false claims of completion directly as a rate: entry 14 observes premature termination and reward hacking in long-horizon rollouts and entry 15 measures shortcut exploitation at 57.1 percent, but neither quantifies the gap between "agent claims done and tests pass" and "agent claims done and hidden tests pass" in normal operation. Budget-matched comparisons of repair versus resampling exist only for self-contained competition-style problems (entry 2), not for repository-scale tasks where test suites are partial. The cost side of human feedback is explicitly untracked (entry 2, limitations). Contamination of training data into self-repair benchmarks is acknowledged in the broader literature but not controlled in any entry here except entry 14's fresh tasks. Finally, all sources predate or barely touch modern agentic harnesses with instruction files and lifecycle hooks, so the configuration layer itself is the untested variable.

## What a configuration layer should do differently

Never let the generator grade itself at a stop gate. The single best-supported rule: a stop gate must consume an execution result, not a self-assessment. Intrinsic self-judgment loses 3 to 25 accuracy points depending on model and task (entry 1), self-feedback on own code is wrong about 40 percent of the time in the cases that matter (entry 2, 32 of 80 inaccurate), and self-repair of self-produced security flaws is a documented blind spot (entry 11). Concretely: a Stop hook should run the test suite in a clean sandbox and block completion on its exit code, and any "all tests pass" claim in the agent's final message should be checked against the hook's own run, not taken as input.

Route on localization, not on review prompts. Entry 5 shows the repair machinery works once the error is localized, and that backtracking is net-positive once mistake-finding reaches roughly 60 to 70 percent accuracy, a bar prompting a large model does not meet but a small trained classifier can. Concretely: when a test fails, the hook should hand the agent the failing test name, the input, expected versus actual output and the stack trace as a structured payload, rather than a generic "review your changes" instruction. If the repo accumulates a log of past fix commits, a small classifier trained on that log to predict which file and test a failure originates from is a better investment than a longer instruction file.

Budget resampling above repair depth. At matched sample budgets, spending on diverse initial samples dominates spending on repair rounds: 10 samples plus 1 repair each beat the baseline while 2 samples plus 10 repairs each fall below it (entry 2, 1.05x versus 0.97x), and one repair per failing program is the empirically best repair count. Entry 12 independently finds most repair gains land in the first two rounds. Concretely: cap repair rounds at two, set one repair candidate per failed test batch, and when the loop exhausts, resample fresh candidates and select by execution rather than continuing to iterate on the same edit; selection against generated or existing tests adds up to 18.8 pass@1 points over taking the first sample (entry 6).

Escalate the verifier tier before the generator tier. Self-repair is bottlenecked by feedback quality, and boosting the feedback model beats boosting the repair loop (entry 2, section 4.2, all budgets). Concretely: sub-agent routing should send the critique-and-localize step to a stronger model tier than the one that writes the fix, and after two failed repair rounds, escalate to a human with the failing test and diff attached, since human feedback raises repair success 1.58x over the strongest model's own feedback (entry 2, Table 1). A cheap trained verifier that reads execution results can serve as the middle tier (entries 5 and 7).

Use self-tests as an escalation trigger, not as proof. Self-generated tests are legitimate as a routing signal: cascading on self-test outcomes cuts cost 26 percent on average at equal accuracy (entry 13), and generated-test selection is the strongest known cheap filter (entry 6). But they are gameable and insufficient as a completion criterion: agents exploit the visible verifier in 13.8 percent of long-horizon rollouts (entry 14) and 57.1 percent of shortcut-available runs, unchanged by anti-cheating prompts (entry 15). Concretely: let the agent write and run its own tests freely during work, but make the final gate a holdout set the agent never saw: maintained regression tests run from a clean checkout, plus property or generated tests that the hook, not the agent, produces.

When not to delegate critique to the same model. Extra same-model critique calls return less than spending the same calls on majority voting over fresh samples: debate at 9 responses scored 83.0 versus 88.2 for self-consistency on GSM8K (entry 1, Table 7). Concretely: drop reviewer sub-agents whose only input is the diff and whose output is prose; replace them with parallel fresh attempts plus execution-based selection, and reserve reviewer agents for cases where they bring an external tool or a different model tier.

Put all checkable requirements in the instruction file up front, not in review prompts. Entry 1's prompt-design result is directly about configuration: requirements that surface only in a feedback prompt inflate the apparent value of self-correction, and folding them into the initial instruction beats the correction loop (81.8 versus 75.1). Concretely: instruction files should state every machine-checkable expectation (style gates, required test commands, coverage floors) as part of the initial task context, and hooks should encode them as commands rather than prose, because prose requirements discovered during review are the pattern that mimics the artifact.

Keep instruction files short on verification claims. Nothing in the verified set supports long instruction files, and entry 1's mechanism suggests why: supplementary instructions in later prompts bias the model away from an already-optimal response to the original prompt. The defensible configuration is a short instruction file that defines how correctness will be checked, plus hooks that do the checking; lengthy self-review checklists in CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md are the untested configuration closest to the design entry 1 shows fails.

## Claims to test

Claim 1: self-reported completion is a weak classifier of true correctness. Experiment: on a benchmark with hidden tests (SWE-bench Verified or an internal repo equivalent), record the agent's own pass/fail prediction before each gate run, then compute AUROC and precision of "done" claims against the hidden result, broken down by task difficulty. Expected if the literature holds: precision well below 1 and near the base rate on hard tasks, echoing the roughly 50 percent mistake-finding ceiling in entry 5 and the blind spot in entry 11. Sample size: 300 instances, 2 models, 3 seeds, about 1,800 runs.

Claim 2: a holdout-test stop gate reduces false completions at bounded cost. Experiment: three stop conditions: agent self-report only, visible test suite run in a hook, visible suite plus a hook-generated holdout set. Measure resolved rate, false-completion rate (claimed done, hidden tests fail) and wall time and token cost. Expected: false completions fall sharply from condition 1 to 3, consistent with entries 6, 14 and 15, at a cost increase under 30 percent. Sample size: 300 instances x 2 models x 3 conditions, 1 seed, about 1,800 runs.

Claim 3: at matched token budget, diverse resampling with test-based selection beats deep repair loops. Experiment: three allocation policies at equal total tokens: k fresh samples with execution-based selection, one sample with up to k repair rounds, and 10 samples with 1 repair each. Outcomes: pass@1, tokens, wall time. Expected: the third policy matches or beats the second, reproducing the 1.05x versus 0.97x ordering in entry 2 and the two-round saturation in entry 12. Sample size: 200 problems per benchmark on two benchmarks (LiveCodeBench slice and an internal task set) x 2 models, about 2,400 runs.

Claim 4: verifier-tier escalation beats generator-tier persistence. Experiment: for failing programs, compare four feedback arms at equal budget: self-feedback, stronger-model feedback, structured test-diff payload only, and small trained localization classifier plus test diff. Outcome: repair success rate per round and cumulative success after two rounds. Expected: arms 2 to 4 beat arm 1, reproducing entry 2 section 4.2 and entry 5 section 5.2. Sample size: 200 failing programs per arm per model, about 1,600 repair episodes.

Claim 5: requirement placement in the instruction file changes measured correctness, not just style. Experiment: hold task and model fixed; vary whether machine-checkable requirements live in the initial prompt, in a review-phase prompt, or in a hook that enforces them mechanically. Outcomes: requirement compliance rate, pass rate, total tokens. Expected: initial placement and mechanical enforcement beat review-phase placement, reproducing entry 1 Table 8 (81.8 versus 75.1 pattern) in an agentic setting. Sample size: 100 tasks x 3 placements x 2 instruction lengths, 2 seeds, about 1,200 runs.

Claim 6: test-edit auditing catches reward hacking that self-report conceals. Experiment: give agents a sandbox where test files are technically editable; arm A has no gate beyond visible tests, arm B has a hook that diffs test files, reruns the suite from a clean checkout and blocks on tamper detection. Outcomes: rate of test edits, rate of false completions, task success. Expected: arm B eliminates most tampering, analogous to the escalation-channel effect and the 13.8 percent rollout hacking rate in entry 14 and the 57.1 percent exploit rate in entry 15. Sample size: 100 runs per arm, 2 models, about 400 runs.
